## 4.2 Refunds and Consumer Protection

Malaysian law gives online buyers some baseline protection. The Consumer Protection (Electronic Trade Transaction) Regulations 2024 took effect on 25 December 2024, replacing the 2012 Regulations. They require online marketplace suppliers to disclose details such as the seller’s identity and contact information, the product description, full price, payment method, terms and estimated delivery time. They also prohibit information known or believed to be false or misleading. Suppliers must provide ways to correct order errors and must acknowledge orders. Operators must provide a complaint channel. Materially different or defective goods must be re-delivered at the supplier’s cost. The Regulations do not, however, promise a refund for every complaint (regs. 1, 3, 5–7).

Abenoh et al. (2019) note that Malaysian food-delivery contracts fall under several laws, including the Consumer Protection Act 1999, the Sale of Goods Act 1957, the Food Act 1983 and the Electronic Commerce Act 2006. They ask how far these laws protect e-consumers, but do not measure refund outcomes. Siali et al. (2024) surveyed 310 food-delivery users in Sarawak, mostly of GrabFood and Foodpanda. They found the right to redress was positively but not significantly related to customer loyalty. They note that platforms handle complaints about late deliveries, incorrect orders and food quality, usually through refund or replacement policies. However, 85.5% of respondents were full-time students and the platforms were large delivery services, so the study does not show how refunds work in a small surplus-food app.

Together, these sources show that disclosure, error correction and redress are recognised concerns in Malaysian food delivery, but they do not tell us what ReMeal offers. We use them to ask what is refundable, when, and how customers can complain. Our conclusions rely on ReMeal’s own terms and seller guides.

## 5.2 Refunds and Cancellations

Under the Terms, customers can cancel and receive a refund only before the vendor accepts the order, and they must request this through the in-app support chat. Once the vendor accepts the order, a customer who cancels is not entitled to any refund, even a partial one (ReMeal, 2024b, §§ 5.7–5.8). Vendors try to follow special instructions but may use their standard procedures instead, and neither ReMeal nor the vendor must refund or replace an order that does not match those instructions (§ 5.2). Eligible refunds go back to the original payment method, although ReMeal may ask for supporting documents first (§ 5.8).

The Terms also set rules for collection. Vendors need only hold an order for 20 minutes after the scheduled collection time (§ 7.2.1). Customers must check their food on collection and report problems before leaving. A customer who causes an unreasonable delay may lose the right to a refund or replacement (§ 7.2.2; Appendix, Fig. A15).

Refunds remain possible in some cases. ReMeal’s seller documentation allows full refunds for cancelled orders and partial refunds when part of an order cannot be fulfilled. Refunds for missing items can also be made after an order is completed, but only ReMeal’s team can process them (ReMeal, n.d.-d).

The key question is whether customers know when they lose the right to cancel. Vendor acceptance decides whether a customer can get a refund. Yet the seller guide lists the order statuses Pending, Open, Collected and Cancelled, with no separate Accepted status (ReMeal, n.d.-c). The policy itself is not necessarily unfair: cancellations after an order has been prepared could cost surplus-food vendors money or waste food. Customers should, however, know what they are agreeing to before paying. This relates to Code Clauses 1.06 and 3.01 on honest communication and making important trade-offs clear (IEEE-CS/ACM Joint Task Force, 1999).

So far, our evidence comes from published policies and seller documentation. We have not tested an actual cancellation or refund. In Part 2, we will check whether checkout shows the refund policy, how the customer app shows vendor acceptance, and how customers report missing or incorrect items. Until then, we cannot conclude that the app misleads customers.
